Humans are suboptimal at correcting robot actions and provide delayed reactions. We analyze real human data to build better benchmarking tools and a better algorithm. With improved benchmarking, we find more reliable rankings in simulation. Our method, Q-OIL, uses these corrections to guide exploration rather than as supervision, reaching 10–50% higher success on real robots (including VLA post-training) with almost 30% less human effort.

Since human corrections are not always optimal, use them for exploration guidance rather than as supervision

We use these insights to design Q-OIL (Q-Optimistic Intervention Learning): a simple change to any off-policy RL algorithm: each takeover earns an optimism bonus that guides exploration.

HIL RL with interventions
  1. for each step:
  2. act: the person if they take over, else the robot
  3. store (s, a, r, s')
  4. update Q toward r + γ Q(s', a')
  5. update the policy to maximize Q

Interventions are treated like any other data.

Q-OIL
  1. for each step:
  2. act: the person if they take over, else the robot
  3. store (s, a, r, s'), plus a bonus b if it was a takeoverFinding 2
  4. update Qtask toward r + γ Qtask(s', a')
  5. update Qopt toward r + b + γ Qtask(s', a')Finding 1
  6. update the policy to maximize Qopt, plus BC on takeoversFinding 3

How does Q-OIL work?

Each takeover earns a small bonus, which raises the value of the path the person showed, so the robot explores corrective behaviors without directly mimicking them. A second, task-only critic provides the bootstrap targets, so the bonus cannot leak back to the states where the robot was failing. As the robot needs help less often, bonus steps become rare and Q-OIL settles on the policy that is best for the task.

Ablation: why two critics?

Q-OIL is stable across a 50× range of bonus values. With one critic, the bonus leaks backward (see Why two critics?), and performance collapses as the bonus grows.
